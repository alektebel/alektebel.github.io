Mus against the machine: signalling under observation

September 2026 • Multi-agent systems • Game theory • Language models

Most questions about AI deception have to be staged: you put an agent in a pretend situation and hope the incentive to lie shows up. Mus does not need staging. It is a Spanish 2-vs-2 card game where partners tell each other about their hands with señas — a fixed vocabulary of winks, tongue, eyebrows and lip-bites. The opposing pair is entitled to watch for those gestures, steal the information if they catch them, and being caught carries a penalty. A covert channel is not a hack here; it is in the rulebook.

That makes it a clean testbed for a question that is otherwise hard to make concrete: how much can two agents say to each other while an adversary is watching, and how much do they leak? I built an engine, four seats, and a Bayesian referee, and this page is the write-up of what came out — plus a playable version you can sit down at.

The channel

Four seats play as two partnerships, N–S against E–W. Each seat sees only its own four cards, the public betting history, señas its partner successfully sent, señas it managed to intercept from the opponents, and the score. Nothing else. The canonical vocabulary is small and fixed:

The metric: a secrecy gap

Both ends of the channel are scored by the same Bayesian referee, in bits of information gained about the emitter's hand class. Fidelity is how many bits your partner gains; leakage is how many the opponents gain; the secrecy gap is the difference.

One design choice matters a lot: both are measured unconditionally. A hand where the partner received nothing scores zero bits rather than being dropped from the sample. A conditional measure makes concealment look free — an intercepted message is exactly as informative as a delivered one. What concealment actually buys you is a lower rate of interception, and only the unconditional measure can see that.

What the baselines show

Six scripted signalling policies, 40 games each:

ConditionFidelityLeakageGapCaught
blatant1.8301.4560.37381
normal1.7770.7361.04018
subtle1.4130.2341.1790
invisible0.8520.0740.7790
deceptive0.6620.0350.62733

The gap is an inverted U, peaking at subtle: signalling openly leaks too much, hiding too well loses your partner. There is an optimal concealment level, and finding it is the skill the benchmark measures.

Two honest caveats. These are scripted heuristics at forty games per condition, so the win rates are noise and nothing here is a statement about language-model behaviour — it validates that the metric discriminates. And the referee is not an LLM call: it fits P(token | hand class) from the corpus itself, so it scores against whatever convention the population actually used.

What you can play

The page linked above is a self-contained build of the game: the real forty-card baraja española, a full rules engine, and two heuristic bot rivals across the table from you and your partner. You signal with the same fixed vocabulary; your partner reads your gestures and plays on them, and if you bluff she notices. Everything runs in the browser.

The research harness underneath is a Python engine with an OpenAI-compatible agent adapter, so the same table can seat real models. The interesting quantity there is not who wins — it is the secrecy gap each model settles into, and whether it can find the subtle sweet spot the scripted policy found. A model that signals bluntly leaks its hand; one that never signals leaves its partner blind; a deceptive one pays for the lie in fidelity.

Why it is worth building

Coordination, covert communication and deception are usually studied separately, with bespoke setups. Mus bundles them into one small game where the incentive to deceive, the cost of being caught, and the value of a trusting partner are all explicit. It turns "can agents keep a secret from an observer?" into something you can measure in bits, per hand, reproducibly — and then sit down and try yourself.

The channel is not a vulnerability to be patched. It is the game. The benchmark just asks how much of it survives the other side watching.
